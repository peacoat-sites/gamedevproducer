Getting the ratio of junior to senior staff wrong is one of the most reliable ways to slow a studio down without knowing why. Too many juniors and your seniors spend their days unblocking people instead of building things. Too many seniors and you're burning budget on people whose skills are better used making decisions than executing tasks someone else could handle at half the cost.

The honest answer on a healthy ratio: most mid-sized [game studios](/posts/best-documentation-tools-for-game-studios/) run roughly 60-65% junior/mid-level and 35-40% senior and above, but that number shifts significantly depending on the type of work and the phase of production. A team deep in live-ops maintenance can tolerate more juniors than a team standing up a new engine or tackling an unproven genre. Context is everything.

## The hidden cost of "cheap" junior hires

A common mistake is treating junior hires as simply cheaper labor. The math looks attractive on paper: hire two juniors for the price of one senior. What doesn't appear on that spreadsheet is the mentorship tax your seniors are now paying.

A skilled senior engineer or artist can typically support 2-3 juniors without it eating their own output. Push that to 4 or 5 and you'll start to see the senior's direct contributions flatten, reviews get rushed, and code quality or asset consistency drifts. The juniors aren't the problem. The structure is.

**Example:** Assume a senior engineer costs $130k/year and a junior costs $70k. You hire three juniors instead of one senior, saving $20k annually. But if each junior requires roughly 25% of a senior's mentoring time, those three juniors consume 75% of one senior's capacity. You haven't saved $20k. You've spent $70k on mentorship hours and produced roughly the same output the senior would have delivered directly, with more coordination overhead and slower iteration. The savings evaporate faster than most hiring managers expect.

This doesn't mean you shouldn't hire juniors. It means you need to account for the absorption cost honestly before you sign the offer letters.

## Senior density by production phase

The right balance isn't fixed; it moves with where you are in development.

| Phase | Recommended Senior % | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-production / R&D | 60-70% | High ambiguity; decisions compound fast; mistakes are expensive to reverse |
| Vertical slice / prototype | 50-60% | Needs experienced judgment; output quality matters for greenlight |
| Full production | 35-45% | Tasks are defined; juniors can execute against clear specs |
| Alpha to gold / crunch | 40-50% | Bug triage and final polish require experienced eyes |
| Live-ops / post-launch maintenance | 25-35% | Stable systems; good documentation lets juniors carry more load |

If you're staffing a new project from scratch, weight senior-heavy at the start and plan your junior hiring ramp to follow. Reversing this, which studios do constantly when they want to "build the team early," just means your juniors are sitting in ambiguity watching seniors figure out what the game actually is.

## Mentorship isn't optional infrastructure

Studios that treat mentorship as something seniors do "if they have time" are going to cycle through juniors every 18-24 months as those people leave for studios [that actually](/posts/game-studio-post-mortem-process-that-actually-works/) develop them. That churn is expensive. Replacing a junior engineer or artist costs somewhere between 50-100% of their annual salary once you factor in recruiting, onboarding, and ramp time.

Structured mentorship doesn't require a formal program with quarterly binders. At a minimum, every junior should have a named senior who owns their growth, a cadence of regular 1:1s, and explicit criteria for what promotion looks like. That last one is what most studios skip. Juniors don't leave because they're unhappy. They leave because they can't see what they're working toward.

One practical approach: a lightweight "promotion rubric" per discipline that lists observable behaviors and deliverables at each level. Art directors and lead engineers usually have this in their heads already. Getting it out of their heads and into a shared doc takes about two hours and pays off every time you're deciding whether to promote someone or making a case to HR for a compensation adjustment.

## Who should NOT be in a senior role

Worth naming: not everyone who's been in the industry for seven years is actually performing at a senior level, and carrying people in senior titles inflates your cost base while reducing the trust juniors have in the system. If the junior can tell the senior is coasting, they notice. If you're paying senior rates for mid-level output because someone has been around long enough, that's a structural problem.

Conversely, some people with four or five years of experience are genuinely operating at a senior level and are being underpaid and undertitled. Those people have options. They'll find studios that recognize what they're doing.

Level calibration across disciplines is worth doing annually. It's uncomfortable. Do it anyway.

## Practical staffing patterns that work

For a team of 20 in full production, a workable distribution might look like: 2 leads (discipline-level ownership), 5-6 seniors (feature owners and decision-makers), 8-9 mid-level (independent contributors, some mentoring), 4-5 juniors (task-level execution with structured support). That's roughly a 45/55 split between mid+ and junior, with leads sitting above the senior tier.

Don't try to run 10 juniors with 2 seniors. You'll see the problem in your sprint velocity within two months.
