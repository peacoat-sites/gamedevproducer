A Next Fest demo is a product in its own right. Players will try it, judge it and drop it exactly as they would a released game, and you get one week to make the impression. Plan it as a launch, not as a build you upload the night before.

Before you start, it helps to know what wishlists are worth to you: the [Steam wishlist calculator](/steam-wishlist-to-sales/) estimates launch-week sales from a wishlist count.

## The dates that decide your plan

Steam Next Fest runs three times a year, in February, June and October. Valve publishes each edition's deadlines on Steamworks, and they arrive earlier than most teams expect.

| Deadline | February 2027 | June 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| Registration closes | Jan 10 (11:59pm PST) | Apr 25 (11:59pm PDT) |
| Valve pulls trailers for the official Next Fest trailer | Jan 18 | May 3 |
| Demo and store page submitted for review, to be live when Press Preview opens | Jan 25 | May 17 |
| All required items submitted for review | Feb 8 | May 31 |
| Press Preview begins | Feb 11 | Jun 3 |
| Festival | Feb 22 to Mar 1 | Jun 14 to 21 |

The October 2026 edition runs October 19 to 26. Every edition and deadline, plus the seasonal sales around them, is on the [Steam sale and Next Fest calendar](/steam-sale-dates/).

To take part, your store page must be published and public, your demo must be playable when the fest starts, and your game must release after the fest ends. It cannot be a prologue or chapter one of an existing game, and each game can only take part in one Next Fest. Because you only get one, choose the edition that lands closest to your launch that you can genuinely be ready for.

## Plan the demo backward from the deadlines

Work back from the Press Preview submission date, not from the festival. A timeline that leaves room for problems:

| Weeks before the fest | Milestone | What "done" means |
|---|---|---|
| 12 | Scope lock | A one-page plan: the hook, the target session length, what is in and out |
| 9 | Demo content complete | Every demo level and system is playable end to end |
| 8 | Cold playtest | Strangers play the demo without help while you watch |
| 7 to 5 | Fix and polish | No new features; only clarity, stability and feel |
| About 4 | Submitted for review | Build and store page in Valve's review, ahead of the Press Preview deadline |
| 2 | Press Preview | Press and creators can play; your outreach is under way |
| 0 | Festival week | Demo public, streams scheduled, team on hand for issues |

Valve's store page and build reviews typically take three to five business days each, and you should plan for at least seven. That is why "submitted for review" sits a week ahead of the deadline that needs it. The [game development schedule planner](/game-development-schedule-planner/) can add a Next Fest to your full production plan and pick the edition that fits your launch date.

## Scope: one hook, delivered completely

A demo is not a vertical slice. A vertical slice is internal proof that your game works. A demo is a standalone experience with one job: to turn a stranger into a wishlist.

Start with the question: what should a player feel in the last five minutes? Then include only what it takes to get them there. One core loop, fully expressed: an opening that teaches it, a middle that tests it, and an ending that makes the player want more.

That means cutting. The crafting system, the second biome, the side mode you are proud of: if they do not serve the hook, they make the demo longer and less clear. Every extra system needs onboarding, and every unexplained one is a place players quit.

End on purpose. A hard stop with a clear wishlist prompt is the minimum. The strongest demos end on tension or curiosity, right before a payoff, so that wishlisting feels like the natural next step.

## Playtesting is not QA

QA finds bugs. Playtesting finds confusion. You need both, at different times.

For Next Fest, run at least one cold playtest: five to ten people who fit your audience and have never seen the game. Communities around similar games are a good place to recruit, and a small gift card is a fair thank-you for their time. Watch them play without explaining anything. Record the session if they agree, or observe live. You are looking for the moment they hesitate, not the moment they say something is confusing, because most players never say it out loud.

When testers do not understand something, fix the clarity of the thing itself before you add tutorial text. A mechanic players cannot read is a design problem, not a writing problem.

## Make the store page do its job

During the fest, your store page is the pitch for the demo. The capsule art and the first seconds of your trailer decide whether someone clicks "Download Demo." If they promise a different game from the one in the demo, you attract players who leave quickly.

Before you submit, lock three things:

- **A short description** that states your genre and hook in one sentence, not your story setup.
- **A first screenshot or GIF** that shows real gameplay at its most readable.
- **A trailer** that leads with how the game feels to play. Story is for people who already care; the trailer's job is to make them care.

Valve pulls trailers for the official Next Fest trailer about five to six weeks before the event, so your best footage needs to be on the page by then. The [Steam page guide](/posts/how-to-build-a-steam-page-that-converts/) goes deeper on capsules, trailers and copy.

## Streaming and creators during the event

Next Fest gives developers a way to broadcast live on their store page during the event. Plan at least two streams: one early in the week to catch the first wave, and one near the end for players who bookmarked the demo. Keep them to an hour or so, and play your own demo while answering chat. That format works because it answers the questions players are forming in real time.

Creator coverage can matter more than anything you broadcast yourself. Press Preview gives press and creators early access to registered demos, so send your outreach before it opens, with a short pitch, a demo key if needed and a clear note about why your game fits their audience.

## After the fest

Look at three things: downloads, how long people played and where they stopped, and wishlists gained during the week. The drop-off point is the most useful number you will get, because it shows exactly where the demo lost people. Fix that before launch, and remember that your launch needs to come after the fest ends.
