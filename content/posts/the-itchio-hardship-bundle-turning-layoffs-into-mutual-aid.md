If you've been following the games industry this year, you already know the mood. Studio closures, mass layoffs, another Monday morning announcement that a team of talented people just lost their jobs. It's exhausting to watch, and for the thousands of developers living it right now, it's genuinely scary. So when Necrosoft Games and the United Videogame Workers-CWA union quietly launched a pay-what-you-want itch.io bundle in late July 2026, it felt different from the usual "thoughts and prayers" energy that follows each new round of cuts. This one had a mechanism. A real one.

The Game Industry Hardship Fund bundle went live with over 100 DRM-free indie games and a $10 minimum buy-in. The money goes directly to the UVW-CWA Game Worker Hardship Fund, which issues grants of up to $5,000 per applicant to US and Canada-based developers facing genuine hardship, covering rent, food, and healthcare. You don't have to be a union member to apply. By August 1, the bundle had already raised $57,859 from 3,984 contributors, averaging $14.52 each, according to Slashdot's coverage that day. By August 4, [Game Developer](/posts/hiring-your-first-game-developer-guide/) reported it had blown past $130,000, more than halfway to the $200,000 goal. The bundle closes August 13. If you're reading this, that's right now.

You might be wondering whether a community fundraiser can actually move the needle on a crisis this size. That's the honest question, and it deserves an honest answer.

## How Bad Is the Layoff Situation, Actually

The numbers have become almost numbing, which is part of the problem. Independent trackers put the 2026 job loss figure at over 9,000 already, pushing the cumulative total since 2022 past 50,000 across the industry. This summer alone, the cuts hit Double Fine, ZA/UM, Romero Games, Lightspeed LA (68 staff in a single wave), and [Ubisoft Barcelona](/posts/shipped-and-fired-what-ubisoft-barcelona-reveals-about-studio-labor/). The 2026 GDC State of the Game Industry report found that more than one in four developers globally had been laid off in the past two years. In the US specifically, the figure climbs to roughly one in three.

That last number is worth sitting with. One in three American [game developers](/posts/contract-basics-for-indie-game-developers/), laid off in two years. These aren't people who made risky bets. Many of them shipped successful games, won awards, built communities. The structural forces that ended their jobs, rising interest rates, publisher consolidation, the post-pandemic correction after studios over-hired, had nothing to do with their individual performance.

## What the Fund Actually Does (and Doesn't Do)

Here's what I tell people when they ask if this kind of mutual aid is meaningful: the goal isn't to solve a structural crisis. It's to keep specific people housed and fed while they figure out what comes next. A $5,000 grant won't replace six months of salary, but it can cover a month's rent in Austin or pay for the prescriptions someone put off buying. That matters.

The UVW-CWA fund is designed for exactly that window, the acute moment between losing a job and finding stability. Grants are available to developers in the US and Canada regardless of union membership, which is a smart design choice. Gatekeeping aid behind union cards would shrink the eligible pool and put an ideological condition on financial desperation. The organizers didn't do that.

What the fund can't do is scale to 9,000 people. Even if the bundle hits $200,000, and distributes every dollar as $5,000 grants, that's 40 developers helped. In the context of the full crisis, 40 is a small number. The organizers know this. The point, as Kotaku noted in their August 4 coverage, is also to demonstrate that organized collective action produces real money fast, and to build the infrastructure for something larger.

Itch.io is the right platform for this kind of thing, not just for ideological reasons but practical ones. The platform already has a culture of pay-what-you-want bundles with social weight behind them. The Bundle for Racial Justice and Equality in 2020 raised over $8 million. Itch's infrastructure handles variable pricing without friction, and its audience skews toward people who actively want to support independent developers. The average contribution of $14.52 when the floor is $10 tells you something real: people are choosing to give more. That's not passive consumption. That's a signal.

## What This Means If You're a Developer Right Now

If you're currently employed in games and watching this from the sidelines, the practical takeaway is simple: buy the bundle before August 13. Ten dollars gets you over 100 games and puts real money into a fund that could be relevant to someone you know, or to you, sooner than you'd like to think. The TechSpot write-up from August 2 put it plainly: this is a low-friction way to participate in direct mutual aid with a concrete outcome.

If you're currently laid off and in the US or Canada, the UVW-CWA Game Worker Hardship Fund is worth applying to regardless of whether you're a union member. The fund exists for food, healthcare, and rent. Those are real categories of crisis for real people right now. You don't need to have done anything wrong to need help, and accepting it isn't a failure.

If you're an indie developer with a game, reaching out to future bundle organizers to get included is a low-cost way to align your work with something that matters to the community. Visibility in a bundle like this carries genuine goodwill.

The bundle closes in days. The crisis doesn't. Whether $200,000 becomes $2 million in future iterations, or whether this stays a meaningful but limited one-time effort, depends partly on how many people treat the deadline as real rather than theoretical. The organizers built something that works. The rest is up to the people who play games and make them.
