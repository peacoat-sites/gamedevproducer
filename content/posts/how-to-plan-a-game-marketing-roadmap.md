Most game marketing roadmaps fail before launch because they're built backward: someone picks a release date, reverse-engineers a few social posts, and calls it a plan. A real marketing roadmap starts with your audience, your platform, and your budget ceiling, then works forward to build momentum that compounds.

The core of a good marketing roadmap is a phased awareness arc: seed the community early, build toward a signal event (a trailer, a demo, a [Steam Next Fest](/posts/how-to-plan-a-game-demo-for-steam-next-fest/) slot), then convert that attention into wishlists or pre-orders, and finally activate your audience at launch. Everything else is execution detail layered on top of that arc.

If you don't know your release window within a 90-day band, your roadmap is guesswork. That doesn't mean you can't start. It means your early phases need to be built around milestones, not calendar dates.

## Who This Is Actually For

This article is aimed at producers and project leads on small-to-mid teams (roughly 5 to 50 people) who are either self-publishing or working with a small publisher where the team itself is driving marketing decisions. If you have a full publisher marketing department running the show, your job is coordination and schedule integration, not roadmap construction. Different problem.

For teams publishing through storefronts directly (Steam, Epic, PlayStation Store, the Nintendo eShop), the producer often ends up owning the marketing calendar by default, because nobody else has the production context to make it realistic.

## Phase Structure: How to Actually Build the Arc

There is no universal number of phases. That said, a structure that works well for most projects 12-24 months from launch looks like this:

**Phase 1: Seeding (12-18 months out).** This is not a marketing phase in the traditional sense. You're establishing presence: a Steam page or comparable storefront listing, a social footprint, and optionally a Discord. The goal is to have something to point to when press or influencers ask. Don't spend serious money here.

**Phase 2: Awareness (6-12 months out).** This is where your first significant trailer or gameplay reveal lives. You need a playable vertical slice or equivalent to make this credible. This phase is where most teams underinvest in follow-up. One trailer is not a campaign.

**Phase 3: Conversion (3-6 months out).** This phase is about turning awareness into wishlists, newsletter signups, or pre-orders. A demo, a Steam Next Fest participation, or a targeted influencer push all serve this goal. This is also where paid advertising starts to make economic sense, because you have a destination to send people.

**Phase 4: Launch activation (0-8 weeks out).** Reviews, influencer embargo lifts, day-one content (patches, streams, AMA). The launch week is a sprint, and it needs its own micro-schedule separate from the roadmap.

**Phase 5: Post-launch sustain (weeks 2-16).** Most roadmaps stop at launch. That's a mistake. Your first content update, your first sale participation, your first port announcement: these are all marketing events. Plan them.

## The Budget Question Nobody Answers Directly

Here's a rough spending framework based on team size and production scale. Note that these are ranges built from common industry practice, not from a single study. Your mileage will vary based on genre, platform mix, and whether you have any existing audience.

| Budget tier | Total prod. budget (approx) | Recommended marketing floor | Key spend areas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Micro-indie | Under $200K | $20K-$40K | Trailer production, 1-2 festival entries, grassroots PR |
| Small indie | $200K-$1M | $80K-$200K | Trailer + localization, influencer seeding, paid social at launch, 1 PR firm |
| Mid-size indie | $1M-$5M | $300K-$750K | Multiple trailers, significant paid media, PR retainer, demo production |
| AA / small publisher | $5M-$20M | $1.5M-$4M | Full media buy, platform co-marketing, regional PR, sustained influencer |

The 15-20% rule (marketing spend as a share of production budget) breaks down at the micro-indie level, where $30K in marketing on a $150K game is a rational floor even though it's 20%. It also breaks down at the AAA level, where the ratio often inverts and marketing exceeds production cost by a wide margin. For the mid-range, the percentage is a decent sanity check.

## Picking Your Signal Events

A signal event is any moment in your calendar designed to generate a measurable spike in attention: wishlists, impressions, press coverage, or social engagement. The roadmap is essentially a chain of signal events with recovery time and content production time built around them.

The best signal events are not purely manufactured. Steam Next Fest, The Game Awards, Gamescom Opening Night Live, PAX demo opportunities: these are calendar anchors that already have audience aggregated around them. Getting your trailer or demo in front of an existing crowd is more efficient than trying to build one from scratch.

**Example:** Assume a 2D action-platformer targeting a 2027 Q2 release on Steam. The team starts building the roadmap 18 months out. Working backward:

- Q4 2025 (18 months out): Steam page live, placeholder capsule art, Discord seeding
- Q1-Q2 2026 (12-15 months out): First gameplay trailer, submitted to 2 indie showcase calls
- Q3 2026 (9 months out): Steam Next Fest demo participation (June or October slot depending on readiness)
- Q4 2026-Q1 2027 (3-6 months out): Launch trailer, influencer outreach, press preview build
- Q2 2027: Launch window, review embargo lift week of release, first post-launch patch

That's five calendar anchors, each requiring a content deliverable. A producer's job is to make sure those deliverables have enough lead time in the production schedule, not just the marketing calendar. A trailer needs 6-10 weeks of production if you're outsourcing. A playable demo needs QA time. These aren't marketing problems; they're production problems that show up in marketing.

## What Gets Cut and What Shouldn't

When budget pressure hits, marketing is often the first thing trimmed. Some of that is rational. A lot of it isn't.

You can cut: paid advertising in Phase 1-2 (the return is poor that early), expensive event booth space if you don't have a demo [that converts](/posts/how-to-build-a-steam-page-that-converts/), printed materials, and wide influencer seeding before your build is representative of the final game.

Don't cut: trailer production quality (a bad trailer is worse than a delayed one), your PR relationship if you've already engaged a firm (breaking that mid-cycle costs you goodwill and re-ramp time), and demo polish if you're committing to a Next Fest slot.

The common misconception is that social media volume can substitute for a real marketing budget. It can't, not for discovery. Social media is good for retaining an existing audience and deepening engagement. It's poor at generating net-new awareness at meaningful scale unless a post goes viral, which you can't plan for.

## Tools Worth Actually Using

For producers building and tracking a marketing roadmap, the toolset doesn't need to be exotic.

**Notion** works well for roadmap documentation and content calendars, especially if the team is already using it for production. Its linked databases let you connect a marketing deliverable back to the production task that feeds it.

**Airtable** is a better fit if your roadmap has a lot of structured tracking (influencer contacts, press list status, asset versions). The relational table model handles that kind of multi-column tracking better than Notion does.

For learning more about the production-marketing interface specifically, Mike Rose has published publicly available talks through GDC that address indie marketing from a producer's operational angle. Rose's talk on "How to Market a Game" (GDC Vault, free tier) is one of the more cited resources in this space. The book "Press Reset" by Jason Schreier doesn't teach marketing directly, but it's instructive on what happens when marketing and production timelines diverge badly at the studio level.

For paid media management, **Google Ads** and **Meta Ads Manager** are the standard platforms. If you're running even a modest launch campaign, learning the basics of UTM tracking and conversion events before you spend money will prevent a lot of waste.
