Most game grants are not lotteries. A funder is buying an outcome: a game that serves its mandate, made by a team that can deliver it, for a budget that holds up. The applications that succeed make that easy to see. The ones that fail usually describe a game the applicant loves without answering what the funder actually asked.

This guide walks through an application step by step. If you are still deciding where to apply, start with the [game development grants database](/game-development-grants/): 22 verified grants, tax credits and rebates across the US, Canada, the UK, the EU, Ireland, France, Germany, the Nordics, Australia and New Zealand, with amounts and current windows.

## Know what kind of money you are asking for

The word "grant" covers three different things, and they need different preparation.

- **Competitive grants** are awarded after an application is assessed against the funder's criteria. The UK Games Fund, Canada Media Fund, Creative Europe MEDIA, France's CNC, Germany's federal games funding and Epic MegaGrants all work this way.
- **Tax credits and rebates** pay a percentage of qualifying spend if you meet the rules, such as the UK's Video Games Expenditure Credit, Ireland's Section 481A credit or Australia's Digital Games Tax Offset. You qualify rather than compete, and you claim after spending.
- **Investment and publishing deals** are not grants at all. They come with recoupment, revenue share or equity. They can be the right choice, but read the terms as a deal, not as free money.

## Step 1: Confirm eligibility before you write anything

Eligibility rules eliminate more applications than reviewers do. Check these first:

- **Location and control.** Most public programs require your company to be based in, and often controlled from, the funding country or province.
- **Stage.** Prototype and development funds want different evidence from production funds. Some funders split streams by stage, such as the Canada Media Fund's Prototyping and Innovation and Experimentation programs, or Ontario Creates' Concept Definition and Production streams.
- **Minimums and caps.** Germany's federal funding starts at projects of €300,000; Ireland's credit needs at least €100,000 of qualifying spend; New Zealand's rebate needs NZ$250,000 a year.
- **Cultural or content tests.** The UK credit requires your game to be certified British; Ireland's requires a cultural certificate.
- **Process requirements.** Some funders require a step before the application. Germany's federal games funding, for example, asks for a draft consultation with its project manager before you submit.

## Step 2: Read the mandate, then answer it

Every funder exists for a reason, and its guidelines say what it is. Cultural funds look for expression of local culture and stories. Innovation funds look for something technically or creatively new. Economic programs look for jobs and spending in the region. Charitable grants, such as Unity for Humanity, look for social impact.

Write to that purpose, using the funder's own evaluation criteria as your headings where possible. Be specific. "Our game explores universal themes" says nothing; a clear description of whose story the game tells, and how it reaches players who would not otherwise see it, says a lot.

## Step 3: Write a production plan a reviewer can believe

Reviewers are judging whether you can finish. A credible plan shows:

- **What already exists.** A prototype, a vertical slice, a playable build or footage is stronger evidence than any description.
- **Milestones with exit criteria.** Not just dates, but what will be true at each one. The [game development schedule planner](/game-development-schedule-planner/) turns a target date into phases and milestones you can lift straight into an application.
- **Who does what.** Name the people in key roles and show relevant experience. If a role is unfilled, say how and when you will fill it.
- **Risks and mitigations.** Naming your biggest risks, and what you will do about them, reads as competence rather than weakness.

## Step 4: Build the budget first, then make the story match it

The budget is where a plan becomes believable or falls apart. Build it line by line before you write the narrative, so the two agree.

- Use realistic rates for your market and show how you got them.
- Cover the costs teams forget: QA, localization if you claim international ambitions, platform and certification costs, marketing, legal and accounting.
- Include contingency, and say what it is for.
- Show your other financing and how the total stays within each program's cap on public funding.

The [indie game budget calculator](/indie-game-budget-calculator/) is a quick way to sanity-check the totals before you build the detailed version.

## Step 5: Show that others believe in it

Where a program accepts them, letters of support from publishers, distributors, platform partners or co-producers show that someone in the industry has looked at the project and sees a market for it. Evidence of an audience helps too: wishlist growth, demo or playtest results, community numbers. Use what you have honestly; inflated claims are easy for experienced reviewers to spot.

## Step 6: Submit early, and plan the time it takes

Deadlines are firm, and portals, registrations and document checks take longer than expected. As a rough guide for a first application, plan for:

| Task | Time to allow |
|---|---|
| Eligibility check and research | 1 to 2 weeks |
| Project description and production plan | 3 to 4 weeks |
| Detailed budget | About 2 weeks |
| Letters of support | 2 to 4 weeks (give people notice) |
| Internal review and revision | 1 to 2 weeks |
| Portal registration and submission | Start early; do not leave it to deadline day |

That is two to three months for a first application done well. If the deadline is closer than that, consider whether a later round would get your best work.

## After you are funded

Funding usually comes with obligations: milestone reports, cost reporting, crediting the funder, and approval for significant changes. Build reporting into your schedule from day one, keep receipts and timesheets organized, and talk to your program officer before changing scope, platforms or key people.

## Common reasons applications fail

- The project does not fit the funder's mandate, however good the game is.
- The budget and the narrative disagree, or the rates are unrealistic.
- There is no evidence the team can deliver.
- Required documents are missing or incomplete.
- The application arrives late, or the portal registration was left until the last day.
