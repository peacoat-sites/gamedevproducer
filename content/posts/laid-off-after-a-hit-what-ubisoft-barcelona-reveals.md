The people who built the underwater levels in *Assassin's Creed Black Flag Resynced* are on strike. The game sold 2 million copies in its first 24 hours. And Ubisoft's response to that success was to propose cutting 51 of the roughly 180 employees at Ubisoft Barcelona, nearly 28% of the studio. If that contradiction doesn't stop you cold, you're not paying close enough attention to what's happening to this industry right now.

The Coordinadora Sindical del Videojuego (CSVI) organized six partial work stoppages starting June 30, 2026, running through July 16. Workers aren't just protesting the layoffs, they're demanding fair severance pay, according to reporting from VGC and IGN. This is the most organized, sustained worker action at a major European [game studio](/posts/how-to-manage-cash-flow-in-a-game-studio/) in recent memory, and it's happening against a backdrop that makes the whole thing feel almost absurd: record-level revenues, mass job losses, and zero apparent connection between the two.

Barcelona isn't an isolated case. It's the sharpest example yet of a pattern that's been building since 2022 and shows no sign of stopping in 2026.

## The Numbers Don't Lie, But They Also Don't Add Up

I'll be honest: I've been in this industry long enough to know that "the game sold well but we're cutting staff anyway" isn't new. What's new is the scale and the brazenness of it. The GDC 2026 State of the Game Industry survey found 1 in 3 U.S. [game developers](/posts/contract-basics-for-indie-game-developers/) had been laid off in the previous two years. One in three. Global gaming revenue is near record highs. Both things are true at the same time.

The Ubisoft Barcelona situation is part of a broader June 2026 restructuring wave that also shuttered the Winnipeg and Belgrade studios entirely, affecting an estimated 380 employees across the company. Then on July 6, Microsoft dropped 3,200 gaming job cuts in a single announcement, the largest single-company gaming layoff in years, while simultaneously divesting four studios: Double Fine, Compulsion Games, Ninja Theory, and Undead Labs. Since 2022, approximately 45,000 [game industry](/posts/associate-producer-game-industry-responsibilities/) jobs have been erased.

## Why "The Game Sold Well" No Longer Protects You

This is the part that should rattle anyone currently employed at a mid-to-large studio. The old mental model was simple: ship a hit, keep your job. Barcelona broke that model in public.

What's actually driving cuts isn't individual game performance. It's portfolio-level accounting, cost-of-capital decisions made at the corporate level, and in Ubisoft's case specifically, years of expensive misses that the company is still paying for. Black Flag Resynced's 2 million day-one sales is genuinely good. But Ubisoft is also still carrying the weight of *Skull and Bones*, *XDefiant*, and other expensive bets that didn't pan out. The people who made the underwater levels don't control any of that math.

What surprised me, researching this, is how little project success insulates individual contributors. Studio executives have known this for years. Most developers find out when HR calls.

## What the Strike Actually Means for Labor in Games

The CSVI action in Barcelona is significant beyond Spain. European studios have stronger labor protections than their North American counterparts, which is partly why this strike was even possible to organize this fast. Six coordinated work stoppages in under three weeks is not nothing. It's a real pressure campaign.

The demands, as reported by IGN, center on fair severance, not on stopping the layoffs entirely. That's a telling choice. The union appears to be fighting for dignified exit terms rather than trying to reverse a corporate decision they probably know is already made. That's pragmatic unionism, and it's the kind of thing that can actually win something concrete.

North American developers are watching. Game Workers Unite and other organizing efforts in the U.S. have made slow progress partly because at-will employment makes collective action legally riskier and culturally harder. But 45,000 layoffs in four years has a way of changing the calculus for people who once thought "just ship good work and you'll be fine."

## The Studio Divestiture Problem Nobody's Talking About Enough

Microsoft's July 6 announcement deserves its own reckoning. Selling or divesting Double Fine, Compulsion, Ninja Theory, and Undead Labs isn't just laying people off. It's restructuring what those studios even are. Some of those developers will end up fine. Some won't. And the studios themselves may emerge smaller, with different mandates, or not at all in their current form.

Here's a quick look at the scope of what's happened across just a few months:

| Event | Date | Jobs Affected |
|---|---|---|
| Ubisoft Barcelona strike begins | June 30, 2026 | 51 proposed cuts |
| Ubisoft closes Winnipeg + Belgrade | June 2026 | ~329 additional (est.) |
| Microsoft gaming layoffs + 4 studio divestitures | July 6, 2026 | 3,200 |
| **Industry total since 2022** | | **~45,000** |

The divestiture model is becoming a way for large publishers to offload studios that don't fit quarterly targets without the full PR hit of a closure. Whether that's better or worse for the people inside depends entirely on who buys the studio and under what terms. Often developers don't know until months after the announcement.

## What This Means If You're Making Games Right Now

I'm not going to tell you the industry is fine. It isn't. But I also don't think the answer is despair or "don't work in games." The answer is clear-eyed realism about where the risk actually lives. Large studios with expensive overhead and corporate parent companies answer to quarterly earnings, not to your shipped credits. That's always been true, but the margin for error has shrunk.

The Ubisoft Barcelona strike is a reminder that collective action is one of the few tools that can create any accountability at all in this dynamic. The CSVI didn't prevent the layoffs, probably. But they're fighting for the people going out the door to leave with something. In 2026, that's not a small thing.

The hit shipped. The workers are still losing their jobs. And a union is the only reason anyone outside Barcelona even knows to be angry about it.
